\section{Accuracy, cost and parallel performance}\label{sec:accuracy_cost}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
% Streams feeding this section: R4 (Hron-Turek FSI3) and R6 (blobInTreacle).
% The 2D block with a flexible tail (Jaiman and Joshi 2022) has no case in
% solids4foam (A1 Section 1.14); it is added only if Philip approves it at G1.

A coupling is more efficient than another only if it reaches the same accuracy at lower cost.
This section compares the complete quasi-monolithic and partitioned IQN-ILS formulations on matched meshes and time steps (their discretisations differ as listed in Section~\ref{sec:consistency_partitioned}), and reports the error against the reference and the cost as a work--precision relation, on two two-dimensional benchmarks and a three-dimensional experiment; a three-dimensional tube then measures how the cost grows with problem size and process count (Section~\ref{sec:scaling_3d}), and Section~\ref{sec:accuracy_cost_discussion} discusses the cost per step across all cases.

\subsection{Turek--Hron FSI3}\label{sec:fsi3_m}

% Data: paper-data/fsi3_runs.csv (fsi3g runs on MeluXina, solids4foam

The FSI3 problem of~\citet{Turek2006}, an elastic flag behind a rigid cylinder oscillating at a solid-to-fluid density ratio of one and $Re = \bar{U}D/\nu_F = 200$, with the mean inflow velocity $\bar{U} = 2\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and the cylinder diameter $D = 0.1\,\mathrm{m}$ (Figures~\ref{fig:fsi3_geom_m} and~\ref{fig:fsi3_field_m}), is the most widely used test of strongly coupled FSI solvers, and its Featflow reference values carry an uncertainty of about $3$--$5\%$ in the displacement and force amplitudes and less than $0.3\%$ in the frequency and mean drag~\citep{CardiffFSIVerification2026}.

\paragraph{Set-up.}
Both couplings are run on one family of three unstructured, predominantly quadrilateral fluid meshes, generated with Gmsh~\citep{Geuzaine2009} and extruded by one cell, with structured layers of cells along the walls, the cylinder and the flag, and refined towards the flag tip; they have $96\,712$, $194\,183$ and $387\,219$ cells (levels 1--3, the nominal cell size reduced by $\sqrt{2}$ per level, with the first-cell size at the flag tip kept at about $0.3\,\mathrm{mm}$).
The flag meshes are uniform block-structured meshes of $430\times24$, $608\times34$ and $860\times48$ cells; the interface is non-matching and is transferred by AMI.
The time steps are $2\times10^{-4}$, $1.41\times10^{-4}$ and $10^{-4}\,\mathrm{s}$, with BDF2 in both regions.
The flow develops for $2\,\mathrm{s}$ around a fixed flag, after which the coupled problem is run to $t = 6\,\mathrm{s}$.
The flag is computed with the geometrically nonlinear velocity solid (quasi-monolithic) and with the geometrically nonlinear displacement solid under IQN-ILS with the strict convergence test of Section~\ref{sec:protocol}, a tolerance of $10^{-6}$ on every residual measure (partitioned).
The quasi-monolithic solid uses the stabilisation of Equation~\eqref{eqn:rhie_chow_s} with $\alpha_d = 0.5$ (Table~\ref{tab:solver_settings}).
The stabilisation vanishes at the design order of the discretisation, so its effect decreases with refinement, but it is noticeable on the level-1 flag: with the jump form of the stabilisation (Section~\ref{sec:discrete_coupled_residual}) and a factor of $0.1$, the lift-amplitude error is $4.0\%$ instead of $6.35\%$, and the errors of the other quantities in Table~\ref{tab:fsi3_m} change by $0.01$--$0.94\%$ of the reference values.

For both couplings the fluid mesh is moved by the linear elastic pseudo-solid assembled on the current mesh, stiffened by the linear inverse distance to the flag and the cylinder (Section~\ref{sec:fluid_mesh_motion}), which keeps the cells next to the flag tip valid throughout the oscillation.
Three further quasi-monolithic runs check the level-2 result: time steps of $10^{-4}$ and $2\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{s}$, and a flag mesh refined twice in each direction.

\paragraph{Measured.}
The mean and amplitude of $u_x$(A), the amplitude and frequency of $u_y$(A), and the mean and amplitude of the drag and lift on the cylinder and flag are evaluated as in the benchmark and the companion paper~\citep{Turek2006, CardiffFSIVerification2026}: the mean and amplitude are half the sum and half the difference of the maximum and minimum over the last full period of the flag motion, and the frequency is averaged over the full periods of the closing second ($t = 5$--$6\,\mathrm{s}$, about five periods); the amplitudes of the last two periods agree with those of the two before to within $0.07\%$ in every run.
The reference values, as in the companion paper, are not the summary values of~\citet{Turek2006}, which differ from them noticeably (for example a drag amplitude of $22.66$ against $27.74\,\mathrm{N/m}$), but the Featflow values tabulated by the benchmark authors for level 4 with $\Delta t = 2.5\times10^{-4}\,\mathrm{s}$, the discretisation of their published reference time history.
Applying the extraction above to that history reproduces the tabulated displacement statistics, mean drag and lift amplitude to within $0.7\%$, the frequencies to within $0.3\%$ and the drag amplitude to within $1.1\%$, which validates the extraction for the quantities compared.
The cost follows the timing protocol of Section~\ref{sec:protocol}: each run is restarted from its state at $t = 5\,\mathrm{s}$ alone on an exclusive MeluXina node with its production number of processes, the first 50 steps are discarded, and the elapsed time of the next flag period ($0.183\,\mathrm{s}$) gives the cost in core-hours per simulated second, the median of three repeats.
The repeats agree within $4\%$, except for IQN-ILS on level 3 ($10\%$) and one repeat of each coupling on level 2, which ran $16$ and $21\%$ slower than the median.

\paragraph{Accuracy.}
At every level both couplings are within the acceptance tolerances of the companion paper (Table~\ref{tab:fsi3_m}): the displacement errors are below $3\%$, the frequency and mean-drag errors below $0.3\%$, and the drag-amplitude errors below $3.2\%$, all inside the uncertainty of the reference.
The lift amplitude has the largest error, $6.4\%$ on level 1, above the uncertainty of the reference, and $2.9$--$3.6\%$ on level 3; it falls under refinement at an observed order of about $1.3$ (quasi-monolithic) and $1.4$ (IQN-ILS), and refining the flag mesh of level 2 twice in each direction lowers it from $4.3$ to $2.5\%$ (the drag-amplitude error from $1.8$ to $1.1\%$, with the $u_y$ amplitude unchanged to $0.05\%$), so the solid mesh accounts for most of the remaining lift error; a high-order reconstruction of the flag lowers the level-1 lift-amplitude error to $1.8\%$ on the same flag mesh, and to $2.4\%$ on a flag mesh with a sixteenth of its cells (Appendix~\ref{app:high_order_solid}).
Reducing or increasing the time step on level 2 by a factor of $\sqrt{2}$ changes every quantity by less than $0.05\%$.
On the same mesh the two couplings differ by $0.04$--$0.65\%$ in the frequency, the mean drag and the $u_y$ and lift amplitudes, and by $0.6$--$1.6\%$ in the $u_x$ statistics and the drag amplitude.
Unlike those of Section~\ref{sec:consistency_partitioned}, these differences do not yet shrink over the three levels (the $u_x$ mean differs by $0.6$, $1.0$ and $1.2\%$), but they stay well inside the reference uncertainty.
They are not caused by the solid time integration: the BDF2 velocity form of the quasi-monolithic solid with BDF2 kinematics is algebraically identical to the BDF2 displacement form of the partitioned solid, for the geometrically nonlinear solid as for the linear one, since both evaluate the stress at the same new-time displacement.
The temporal differences between the couplings are in the fluid and at the interface: the quasi-monolithic fluid moves its mesh once per step rather than in every coupling iteration, and takes its wall velocity from the solid rather than from the mesh motion (Table~\ref{tab:discretisation_differences}).
These are consistent differences of order $\Delta t^2$, which vanish as the time step is reduced, and the quasi-monolithic time-step checks above suggest that they are small here (the temporal error of the partitioned solution was not measured separately).
The remaining differences are therefore attributed mainly to the spatial differences of Table~\ref{tab:discretisation_differences}, such as the interface pressure condition, which have not yet started to decrease over three levels refined by only $\sqrt{2}$ each.
Over the three levels the $u_x$ mean changes by $1.3\%$ and then $0.05\%$ (quasi-monolithic), and by $1.7\%$ and then $0.14\%$ (IQN-ILS), so the two couplings converge at a similar rate.

\paragraph{Cost.}
The quasi-monolithic coupling needs two Newton iterations per step at every level, with $46$, $54$ and $67$ Krylov iterations per Newton iteration on levels 1--3, and IQN-ILS needs $4.9$--$6.0$ coupling iterations per step.
On the same processes, one Newton iteration costs as much as $3.0$, $3.5$ and $5.0$ coupling iterations on levels 1--3.
This ratio is larger than on the other two-dimensional cases, and it rises with the mesh, as do the Krylov iterations per Newton iteration.
Per simulated second, the quasi-monolithic coupling is therefore $1.2$, $1.3$ and $1.6$ times more expensive than IQN-ILS on levels 1--3 (Figure~\ref{fig:fsi3_work_precision}).
On the same mesh the two couplings reach errors that are indistinguishable within the reference uncertainty, so the quasi-monolithic coupling costs $1.2$--$1.6$ times as much as IQN-ILS for an accuracy indistinguishable from it against this reference, the factor rising with refinement; below errors of about $3$--$5\%$, every quantity except the lift amplitude is limited by the reference rather than by either coupling.

The quasi-monolithic coupling is not more expensive because of its nonlinear convergence: two Newton iterations per step is close to the minimum for a Newton method.
Its cost lies in each Newton iteration, which solves one preconditioned linear system for all fluid and solid unknowns, whereas a partitioned coupling iteration performs one segregated (PIMPLE) fluid solve and one solid solve.
The fluid mesh has about ten times as many cells as the flag mesh, so the fluid block is expected to dominate this linear system and the growth of its Krylov iterations with the mesh to set the growth of the cost ratio, although the quasi-monolithic cost was not profiled by block.
To separate the effect of the fluid solver from that of the coupling, the level-1 case was also run with IQN-ILS using the coupled Newton fluid solver of the quasi-monolithic method in place of the PIMPLE solver, with the same fluid discretisation, preconditioner and tolerances, restarted from the developed state at $t = 5\,\mathrm{s}$ on 16 processes of a workstation (Apple M1 Ultra).
Its tip displacement $u_y$ matches that of IQN-ILS with the PIMPLE solver to $0.03\%$ of the amplitude, and the drag and lift to $0.1$--$0.4\%$.
One Newton step of the fluid costs about six times as much as one PIMPLE fluid solve ($0.65$ against $0.11\,\mathrm{s}$), and about as much as one Newton iteration of the quasi-monolithic coupling, which includes the solid ($0.61\,\mathrm{s}$).
With this fluid solver IQN-ILS needs $6.9$ coupling iterations per step when the fluid is solved with one Newton step per coupling iteration, and $5.6$ coupling iterations of $2.2$ Newton steps each when it is converged; per step it performs about $440$ and $810$ Krylov iterations against $91$ for the quasi-monolithic coupling, and costs four to seven times as much.
The timings come from short windows on a shared machine, using only steps during which no other job ran, and are therefore approximate; the Krylov iteration counts are not affected by the machine load.
In this test, therefore, the extra cost of the quasi-monolithic coupling relative to IQN-ILS with the PIMPLE solver comes mainly from the coupled Newton solution of the fluid equations rather than from the fluid--solid coupling: with the same fluid solver the quasi-monolithic coupling is the cheaper of the two.
% Data: claude-runs/newton-partitioned/RESULTS.md (build a421f063d, Mac Studio, 17 Nov 2026).
Faster solution of the coupled fluid block is therefore the most promising route to a cheaper quasi-monolithic coupling; preconditioners based on segregated pressure-correction (SIMPLE-type) algorithms have been proposed for this purpose~\citep{Pernice2001, Knoll2004, Klaij2013}, but such a preconditioner did not reduce the cost in initial tests, and it is left for future work.

\citet{Liu2014} proposed an elastic half-cylinder attached to the floor of a highly viscous channel flow (Figure~\ref{fig:blob_geom_m}) to measure the temporal order of their quasi-monolithic finite element formulation, on which the present method is based~\citep{JaimanJoshi2022}.
Repeating their temporal study with the present cell-centred finite volume formulation is therefore the most direct comparison with the finite element method; the companion paper uses the case only for partitioned self-convergence~\citep{CardiffFSIVerification2026}.

\paragraph{Set-up.}
An elastic half-cylinder of radius $0.5\,\mathrm{m}$ ($\lambda_s = 500\,\mathrm{Pa}$, $\mu_s = 50\,\mathrm{Pa}$, $\rho_s = 1\,\mathrm{kg/m^3}$) is immersed in a fluid of the same density and kinematic viscosity $1\,\mathrm{m^2/s}$, so that $Re = U_\mathrm{max} D/\nu_F \approx 1$, with the peak inlet velocity $U_\mathrm{max} = 1.125\,\mathrm{m/s}$ and the half-cylinder diameter $D = 1\,\mathrm{m}$, and the added mass is strong.
A parabolic inlet velocity is ramped up over $2\,\mathrm{s}$, after which the flow becomes steady.
\citet{Liu2014} use a small-strain linear elastic solid, but the top of the half-cylinder moves by more than a quarter of its radius, and a small-strain solid integrates the fluid traction over its undeformed surface, so that it receives about $12\%$ less force than the fluid exerts~\citep{CardiffFSIVerification2026}.
As in the companion paper, the solid is therefore finite strain, with the compressible neo-Hookean strain energy $\Psi = \tfrac{\mu_s}{2}(\bar{I}_1 - 3) + \tfrac{K_s}{4}(J^2 - 1 - 2\ln J)$, $K_s = \lambda_s + \tfrac{2}{3}\mu_s$, which reduces to Liu et al.'s model for small strains: the geometrically nonlinear velocity solid (quasi-monolithic) and the total Lagrangian displacement solid (partitioned).
Both regions use BDF2 in time.
% The quasi-monolithic variant uses backward for the fluid and the solid since
% solids4foam 6dc42799b (Euler for the fluid before, e.g. at 257b2e564).

\paragraph{Measured.}
The fluid and solid meshes are block structured, with matching interface faces.
The temporal study follows~\citet{Liu2014} and the partitioned study of the companion paper: on the coarsest mesh (factor 1; 480 fluid and 120 solid cells), $\Delta t = 0.25$ to $0.015625\,\mathrm{s}$, halved each time, to $t = 1\,\mathrm{s}$, monitoring the self-convergence of the top-point displacement $u_x$(A) and the interface position.
The quasi-monolithic coupling uses Newton tolerances of $10^{-8}$ and a Krylov tolerance of $10^{-6}$, so that its iterative error lies below the smallest change; IQN-ILS uses the strict test of Section~\ref{sec:protocol} with a tolerance of $10^{-6}$, without reusing the modes of earlier time steps.
The mesh study computes the steady $u_x$(A) on mesh factors 1, 2 and 4 ($\Delta t = 0.01$, $0.01$ and $0.005\,\mathrm{s}$) with the default tolerances of Table~\ref{tab:solver_settings} (quasi-monolithic) and a coupling tolerance of $10^{-6}$ (IQN-ILS) to $t = 3\,\mathrm{s}$, by which time it varies by less than $2\times10^{-4}$ of its value over the last $0.5\,\mathrm{s}$.
The temporal study of both couplings is timed serially under the protocol of Section~\ref{sec:protocol}, over each whole run excluding its first step; the mesh-study runs share one node, so their times are indicative.

\paragraph{Temporal convergence.}
The quasi-monolithic sequence is broadly compatible with second-order convergence (Figure~\ref{fig:blob_time}), with observed orders of 2.07 and 3.22 for $u_x$(A) and 3.66, 2.62 and 2.65 for the interface position, consistent with the second order reported by~\citet{Liu2014} for their finite element formulation; the last change in $u_x$(A), $7\times10^{-6}\,\mathrm{m}$, approaches the level of the Newton tolerance (order 1.20).
IQN-ILS converges at a similar rate (orders 2.13 and 2.21 for $u_x$(A), 2.12, 2.00 and 2.63 for the interface), but its last change in $u_x$(A), $8\times10^{-7}\,\mathrm{m}$, is smaller than second order predicts and depends on the coupling tolerance (with $10^{-7}$ the last order is 1.44), so at the smallest steps its iterative error is comparable to the temporal change.
The quasi-monolithic coupling needs 2.0--3.0 Newton iterations per step and IQN-ILS 11.0--14.5 coupling iterations (at most 22); the five quasi-monolithic runs take $21\,\mathrm{s}$ in total against $118\,\mathrm{s}$ for IQN-ILS, 5.6 times less, despite their tighter tolerances.
At the smallest step the couplings differ by $0.09\%$ in $u_x$(A), close to their steady-state difference on this mesh, which is therefore spatial rather than temporal.

\paragraph{Mesh convergence.}
At steady state (Figure~\ref{fig:blob_field}) the flow accelerates over the half-cylinder, which leans downstream by about $0.14\,\mathrm{m}$ at its top.
Both couplings converge at close to second order in space (Table~\ref{tab:blob_mesh}); their difference changes sign between factors 1 and 2 and falls to $0.05\%$ on factor 4 and to $0.02\%$ after extrapolation, so they converge to the same solution.
The quasi-monolithic coupling needs 2.0, 2.1 and 2.7 Newton iterations per step on the three meshes, with 24, 38 and 65 Krylov iterations per Newton iteration, whereas IQN-ILS needs 9.1, 10.3 and 12.2 coupling iterations per step (at most 14, 16 and 18), so that the quasi-monolithic runs are two to four times faster.
In both studies IQN-ILS uses the settings of the companion paper, which do not reuse the modes of earlier time steps; reusing them generally reduces the number of coupling iterations, so the cost ratios hold for these settings.
