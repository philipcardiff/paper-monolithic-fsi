\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusion}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%

This paper has presented a quasi-monolithic cell-centred finite volume method for fluid--solid interaction, in which the ALE fluid equations and the total Lagrangian solid momentum equation in velocity form are solved as one nonlinear system by a Jacobian-free Newton--Krylov method, with the fluid mesh motion outside the Newton system~\citep{Liu2014, JaimanJoshi2022}.
It was verified against exact solutions, compared with the partitioned solver of solids4foam on matched meshes and time steps, and assessed as a complete solver against partitioned couplings at matched verified error~\citep{CardiffFSIVerification2026}.
The main conclusions are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \emph{The compact finite volume linearisations are adequate as a preconditioner but not as a Jacobian.} As the Newton operator, the assembled approximate Jacobian converges only linearly (contraction factors $0.2$--$0.85$), whereas the matrix-free operator needs one to four Newton iterations per step in every completed run (one only on the steady Hessenthaler flap). The field-split preconditioner needs 14--30 Krylov iterations per Newton iteration on the ring and the tubes and 180--260 on the thin wall of the collapsible channel (Sections~\ref{sec:robustness} and~\ref{sec:tube3d_pc_study}).
	\item \emph{On thin, stiff walls the cost of the linear solve is set by the solid block of the preconditioner.} The compact solid block treats the velocity components separately and misrepresents bending, so that the Krylov iterations grow with the slenderness and the stiffness of the wall; with the Jacobian of the solid residual, computed once by coloured finite differences, as the solid block, the collapsible channel needs 20--28 Krylov and two Newton iterations per step for walls of slenderness 50--400, and its run time falls by a factor of 11.6 at an unchanged solution (Sections~\ref{sec:solid_block} and~\ref{sec:robustness_collapsible}).
	\item \emph{The interface pressure condition strongly influences both the frequency and the damping of the added-mass response.} With the interface pressure gradient taken from the normal momentum equation, the ring reproduces the exact added-mass frequency ratio at observed orders of 3.2 and above and adds no measurable damping; a zero normal gradient reduces the order to first and adds an amplitude loss of up to $2.3\%$ per period (Sections~\ref{sec:ring_m} and~\ref{sec:interface_comparison}). On a deforming wall, the traction must include the transpose part of the viscous stress (Section~\ref{sec:womersley_consistency}).
	\item \emph{The transpose transfer balances the interface force to round-off on non-conformal interfaces and lets the solid be refined independently.} The force imbalance falls from $3$--$9\times10^{-5}$ with interpolated traction to below $3\times10^{-15}$, and a $640\times8$ solid through AMI brings the collapsible-channel trough within $0.52\%$ of the oomph-lib reference, against $0.98\%$ for a conformal solid on a finer fluid mesh (Sections~\ref{sec:interface_comparison} and~\ref{sec:robustness_collapsible}).
	\item \emph{The quasi-monolithic and partitioned solutions converge towards each other under refinement.} On the finest meshes they differ by $0.007\%$ in the cavity displacement and by at most $0.13\%$ in the tube displacement and timing, and on the elastic half-cylinder their extrapolated values agree within $0.02\%$; on the three-dimensional Hessenthaler flap the closing-window tip deflections differ by $2.1$, $0.25$ and $0.5\%$ on three meshes, and both couplings match the measured flap centreline to $0.2$--$0.3\,\mathrm{mm}$ RMS (Sections~\ref{sec:consistency_partitioned}, \ref{sec:blob_m} and~\ref{sec:hessenthaler_m}).
	\item \emph{The Newton iteration count is insensitive to the added mass over the tested range.} The method completes every cell of the ring and collapsible-channel maps, including the massless wall, with 2--3.1 Newton iterations per step, where, for density ratios of 0.1 and below, Aitken and IQN-ILS reach their iteration cap in 12 and 8 of 12 cells (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}).
	\item \emph{Whether the method is cheaper depends on how many coupling iterations the partitioned scheme needs, and on the cost of the linear solve in each Newton iteration.} One Newton iteration costs about 1.1 IQN-ILS coupling iterations on the half-cylinder, 1.5--1.6 on the ring and 3.0--5.0 on the FSI3 meshes. The method is 1.4--1.7 times cheaper on the ring and two to six times on the half-cylinder, where added mass is strong, and 3.2--3.5 times dearer than Robin--Neumann on the three-dimensional tube, on which it keeps a single-node parallel efficiency of 0.81 on 128 processes. On Turek--Hron FSI3, where IQN-ILS needs five to six coupling iterations per step, it costs 1.2--1.6 times as much as IQN-ILS on the same meshes, at an accuracy indistinguishable within the uncertainty of the Featflow reference, the factor rising with refinement (Sections~\ref{sec:accuracy_cost} and~\ref{sec:scaling_3d}). On the three-dimensional Hessenthaler flap, where IQN-ILS needs about four coupling iterations per step on the two meshes compared, it costs 1.9 times as much on level 1, where the flap keeps a small oscillation and Newton needs two iterations per step, and about as much (0.92--1.08 times) on level 2, where the steady flap needs one, on the same processes (Section~\ref{sec:hessenthaler_m}). These comparisons hold for the partitioned parameters of the companion paper and the linear solver settings used (Section~\ref{sec:accuracy_cost_discussion}).
\end{enumerate}

The method has limitations that also point to future work.
Its cost is that of the linear solve in each Newton iteration.
On thin walls whose stiffness dominates their inertia over a time step, this cost is set by the compact solid block of the preconditioning matrix, which the Jacobian of the solid residual removes; on compact solids that block is adequate, and the denser alternative can be dearer (Section~\ref{sec:tube3d_pc_study}), so the choice between them, made automatically by timing in the implementation, depends on the structure.
On large three-dimensional meshes the single-level ILU(0) preconditioning of the fluid velocity block lets the Krylov iterations grow with the mesh, as on the Turek--Hron FSI3 meshes, where the solid block does not matter, and the replicated LU factorisation of the solid block, robust and cheap for the small solid blocks of the two-dimensional cases, does not scale, so that algebraic multigrid is used for the solid of the three-dimensional tube.
A faster fluid-block preconditioner, for example one based on a segregated pressure-correction algorithm or on algebraic multigrid for the velocity block, assessed on large three-dimensional cases, is therefore the most direct route to a cheaper method there.
The row scaling of the residual also deserves attention: with the factors used here the Newton test is met by the fluid rows, and the solid residual is reduced less than the tolerance suggests, although the wall displacements remain converged to about $10^{-6}$; scaling both regions to forces makes the test apply to both (Section~\ref{sec:jfnk_solution}).
Like any Newton method without globalisation, the method may also fail to converge over a time step in which the coupled problem is strongly nonlinear, for example through a start-up; a smaller time step then helps, and a globalisation such as pseudo-transient continuation~\citep{Knoll2004} is a natural extension.
The fluid mesh moves outside the Newton system, so the method inherits the limits of its mesh-motion solver, which must be chosen for the largest interface increment per step and the smallest cells next to the interface (Section~\ref{sec:fluid_mesh_motion}).

Beyond these, the main extension is towards truly higher-order FSI solvers, both quasi-monolithic and partitioned, with high-order discretisations of the solid, the fluid and the interface transfer: the high-order solid residual already reduces the solid mesh needed for thin structures (Appendix~\ref{app:high_order_solid}), and a high-order fluid discretisation is under development.
Larger three-dimensional cases and moving bodies with large displacements are a further target: since the mesh motion is already outside the Newton system, the method may be combined with overset meshes~\citep{miller2014overset} or with immersed boundary methods~\citep{peskin2002immersed}, for which an immersed-boundary form of the Turek--Hron FSI2 benchmark is already available in solids4foam~\citep{solids4foamImmersedFSI2}.
