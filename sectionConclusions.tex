This paper has presented a quasi-monolithic cell-centred finite volume method for fluid--solid interaction, in which the ALE fluid equations and the total Lagrangian solid momentum equation in velocity form are solved as one nonlinear system by a Jacobian-free Newton--Krylov method, with the fluid mesh motion outside the Newton system~\citep{Liu2014, JaimanJoshi2022}.
It was verified against exact solutions, compared with the partitioned solver of solids4foam on matched meshes and time steps, and assessed as a complete solver against partitioned couplings at matched verified error~\citep{CardiffFSIVerification2026}.
The main conclusions are as follows.

\begin{enumerate}
	\item \emph{The compact finite volume linearisations are adequate as a preconditioner but not as a Jacobian.} As the Newton operator, the assembled approximate Jacobian converges only linearly (contraction factors $0.2$--$0.85$), whereas the matrix-free operator needs two to four Newton iterations per step in every completed run. The field-split preconditioner needs 14--30 Krylov iterations per Newton iteration on the ring and the tubes and 210--310 on the thin wall of the collapsible channel (Sections~\ref{sec:robustness} and~\ref{sec:tube3d_pc_study}).
	\item \emph{The interface pressure condition strongly influences both the frequency and the damping of the added-mass response.} With the interface pressure gradient taken from the normal momentum equation, the ring reproduces the exact added-mass frequency ratio at observed orders of 3.2 and above and adds no measurable damping; a zero normal gradient reduces the order to first and adds an amplitude loss of up to $2.3\%$ per period (Sections~\ref{sec:ring_m} and~\ref{sec:interface_comparison}). On a deforming wall, the traction must include the transpose part of the viscous stress (Section~\ref{sec:womersley_consistency}).
	\item \emph{The transpose transfer balances the interface force to round-off on non-conformal interfaces and lets the solid be refined independently.} The force imbalance falls from $3$--$9\times10^{-5}$ with interpolated traction to below $3\times10^{-15}$, and a $640\times8$ solid through AMI brings the collapsible-channel trough within $0.71\%$ of the oomph-lib reference, against $1.17\%$ for a conformal solid on a finer fluid mesh (Sections~\ref{sec:interface_comparison} and~\ref{sec:robustness_collapsible}).
	\item \emph{The quasi-monolithic and partitioned solutions converge towards each other under refinement.} On the finest meshes they differ by $0.055\%$ in the cavity displacement and by at most $0.13\%$ in the tube displacement and timing, and on the elastic half-cylinder their extrapolated values agree within $0.02\%$; on the three-dimensional Hessenthaler flap the closing-window tip deflections differ by $2.1$ and $0.25\%$ on two meshes, and both couplings match the measured flap centreline to $0.2$--$0.3\,\mathrm{mm}$ RMS (Sections~\ref{sec:consistency_partitioned}, \ref{sec:blob_m} and~\ref{sec:hessenthaler_m}).
	\item \emph{The Newton iteration count is insensitive to the added mass over the tested range.} The method completes every cell of the ring and collapsible-channel maps, including the massless wall, with 2--3.2 Newton iterations per step, where, for density ratios of 0.1 and below, Aitken and IQN-ILS reach their iteration cap in 11 and 7 of 12 cells (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}).
	\item \emph{Whether the method is cheaper depends on how many coupling iterations the partitioned scheme needs, and on the cost of the linear solve in each Newton iteration.} One Newton iteration costs about 1.1--1.2 IQN-ILS coupling iterations on the ring and half-cylinder and 3.0--5.0 on the FSI3 meshes. The method is two to six times cheaper where added mass is strong (ring, half-cylinder), and 3.2--3.6 times dearer than Robin--Neumann on the three-dimensional tube, on which it keeps a single-node parallel efficiency of 0.78 on 128 processes. On Turek--Hron FSI3, where IQN-ILS needs five to six coupling iterations per step, it costs 1.2--1.6 times as much as IQN-ILS on the same meshes, at an accuracy indistinguishable within the uncertainty of the Featflow reference, the factor rising with refinement (Sections~\ref{sec:accuracy_cost} and~\ref{sec:scaling_3d}). On the three-dimensional Hessenthaler flap, where IQN-ILS needs about four coupling iterations per step on the two meshes compared, it costs 2.0 and 6.0 times as much on the same processes: Newton still converges in two iterations per step, but the Krylov iterations per Newton iteration of the linear solver, which was not optimised for this case, double between the meshes (Section~\ref{sec:hessenthaler_m}). These comparisons hold for the partitioned parameters of the companion paper and the linear solver settings used (Section~\ref{sec:accuracy_cost_discussion}).

\end{enumerate}

The method has limitations that also point to future work.
Its cost is that of the linear solve in each Newton iteration, most likely dominated by the fluid block; on large three-dimensional meshes the single-level ILU(0) preconditioning of the fluid velocity block lets the Krylov iterations grow with the mesh, and the replicated LU factorisation of the solid block, robust and cheap for the small solid blocks of the two-dimensional cases, does not scale, so that algebraic multigrid is used for the solid of the three-dimensional tube.
A faster fluid-block preconditioner, for example one based on a segregated pressure-correction algorithm or on algebraic multigrid for the velocity block, assessed on large three-dimensional cases, is therefore the most direct route to a cheaper method.
Like any Newton method without globalisation, the method may also fail to converge over a time step in which the coupled problem is strongly nonlinear, for example through a start-up; a smaller time step then helps, and a globalisation such as pseudo-transient continuation~\citep{Knoll2004} is a natural extension.

The fluid mesh moves outside the Newton system, so the method inherits the limits of its mesh-motion solver, which must be chosen for the largest interface increment per step and the smallest cells next to the interface (Section~\ref{sec:fluid_mesh_motion}).

Beyond these, the main extension is towards truly higher-order FSI solvers, both quasi-monolithic and partitioned, with high-order discretisations of the solid, the fluid and the interface transfer: the high-order solid residual already reduces the solid mesh needed for thin structures (Appendix~\ref{app:high_order_solid}), and a high-order fluid discretisation is planned.
Larger three-dimensional cases and moving bodies with large displacements are a further target: since the mesh motion is already outside the Newton system, the method may be combined with overset meshes~\citep{miller2014overset} or with immersed boundary methods~\citep{peskin2002immersed}, for which an immersed-boundary form of the Turek--Hron FSI2 benchmark is already available in solids4foam~\citep{solids4foamImmersedFSI2}.
