\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
Fluid--solid interaction (FSI) problems in which an incompressible fluid interacts with a light or flexible solid are strongly coupled: the fluid pressure acts on the solid as an added mass, and the coupled problem cannot be advanced reliably by solving the two fields one after the other.
Cell-centred finite volume (FV) methods are widely used for both the fluid and, increasingly, the solid, yet FV FSI solvers are almost always partitioned, whereas monolithic and quasi-monolithic Newton-type solvers have been developed almost exclusively with finite elements.
This paper presents a quasi-monolithic cell-centred FV method for FSI, solved with a Jacobian-free Newton--Krylov (JFNK) procedure and implemented in solids4foam~\citep{Cardiff2025}, an open-source solid mechanics and FSI toolbox built on the finite volume library OpenFOAM~\citep{Weller1998}, and asks two questions of it: is the method consistent with the governing equations and with the established partitioned solver, and what does it cost and gain relative to partitioned coupling at the same verified accuracy?

FSI methods can be classified by the modelling viewpoint, the degree of interface coupling and the solver structure.
Eulerian approaches treat both domains as ``fluid-like'', e.g.\ volume-of-fluid~\citep{jain2019conservative} and level-set methods~\citep{dunne2006eulerian, cottet2008eulerian}, and Lagrangian (particle) approaches treat both as ``solid-like''~\citep{aubry2005particle, aubry2006fractional, cremonesi2020state}; the former make sharp, conservative interfaces and general solid constitutive laws difficult, and the latter are typically expensive.
Mixed approaches treat the solid as Lagrangian and the fluid with an arbitrary Lagrangian--Eulerian (ALE) description, which is effective for small to moderate interface motion but fails for very large deformations~\citep{wall2006large}; immersed boundary~\citep{peskin1977numerical, peskin2002immersed, iaccarino2003immersed}, overset~\citep{ge2003three, miller2014overset, li2021fluid, cane2021patient} and cut-cell methods~\citep{pasquariello2016cut, tao2020new, xie2020cartesian} relax this restriction at the price of more complex interface treatment.
The present work uses a body-fitted ALE fluid and a total Lagrangian solid.

Weakly coupled (loosely coupled) schemes solve each field once per time step~\citep{farhat2000two} and are unstable when the added mass is large~\citep{Causin2005, Forster2006}, as for incompressible flow interacting with light, flexible solids.
Strongly coupled schemes enforce the interface kinematic and dynamic conditions at each time step, either by iterating between separate fluid and solid solvers (partitioned coupling) or by solving the fields as one system (monolithic coupling).
Partitioned schemes keep the field solvers and their discretisations separate~\citep{Tukovic2018} and are accelerated by Aitken relaxation~\citep{irons1969version, kuttler2008fixed}, interface quasi-Newton methods such as IQN-ILS (interface quasi-Newton with an approximation of the inverse Jacobian from a least-squares model)~\citep{Degroote2009, bungartz2016precice, santiago2020hpc, delaisse2022surrogate}, or Robin-type interface conditions~\citep{Tukovic2018b}.
Their convergence deteriorates as the added mass grows, and Dirichlet--Neumann iterations cannot converge for an incompressible fluid fully enclosed by Dirichlet boundaries and the interface~\citep{Kuttler2006}.
Monolithic schemes avoid outer iterations and are more robust under strong added mass, at the cost of modularity and of a large, poorly conditioned linear system~\citep{Degroote2009, Heil2008}.

Monolithic FSI has been developed predominantly with finite elements.
\citet{Heil2004} showed that block-triangular approximations of the coupled Jacobian are effective preconditioners for monolithic ALE FSI solved with GMRES, and \citet{Heil2008} compared monolithic and segregated solution of the same discretisation.
\citet{Mayr2015} developed a temporally consistent monolithic Newton--Krylov formulation with block preconditioning, and fully coupled Newton--Krylov methods with multigrid and Schwarz preconditioners have since been demonstrated~\citep{Aulisa2018}, alongside block preconditioners designed for added-mass-dominated problems~\citep{Badia2008, Muddle2012, Deparis2016, Jodlbauer2019, Calandrini2020}.
\citet{Liu2014} introduced a quasi-monolithic formulation in which the fluid mesh motion is moved outside the coupled Newton system, retaining strong coupling of the fluid and solid unknowns and second-order temporal accuracy under strong added mass; \citet{JaimanJoshi2022} give a textbook account of the resulting algorithm, which the present method follows.
The monolithic FV/FE method of \citet{Eken2016} is the closest in solver architecture: it couples a side-centred (staggered) unstructured FV fluid to a Galerkin FE solid and solves the coupled system with inexact Newton, FGMRES and a block upper-triangular preconditioner in PETSc.

Body-fitted cell-centred FV FSI, particularly within OpenFOAM, has instead followed partitioned coupling.
The partitioned framework of \citet{Tukovic2018} underpins solids4foam~\citep{Cardiff2025} and much subsequent applied work, and coupling libraries such as preCICE~\citep{bungartz2016precice} extend the same paradigm across codes.
The vertex-centred hybrid scheme of \citet{Malan2013} achieves strong coupling by dual-time iteration that remains block-Jacobi at the FSI level.
Unified FV formulations, beginning with \citet{Greenshields1999b} and \citet{Greenshields2005} and continued by \citet{Giannopapa2006, Giannopapa2008} and \citet{Tandis2023}, solve common equations across the fluid and solid regions, but with segregated pressure--velocity algorithms rather than a coupled Newton solve.
For cell-centred FV solids, the authors have recently shown that JFNK with a compact approximate Jacobian as preconditioner converges robustly~\citep{Cardiff2026, Batistic2026}; the present work extends that approach to the coupled FSI system.

Three aspects of a monolithic cell-centred FV formulation are not settled by the finite element literature above.
The first is the Newton operator: compact FV linearisations omit contributions such as the explicit non-orthogonal corrections and the higher-order reconstructions that the residual contains, so they are natural preconditioners but poor Jacobians, and JFNK~\citep{Knoll2004} removes the need to differentiate the discretisation exactly.
The second is the interface treatment: in a collocated scheme the interface pressure and traction are boundary values evaluated from cell-centred fields, and a boundary condition that is adequate for a partitioned solver can limit the order of accuracy of the coupled solution; the companion verification study traced a first-order displacement convergence in the Hron--Turek FSI3 problem~\citep{Turek2006} partly to a first-order wall-pressure condition~\citetodo{companion verification paper}.
The third is non-conformal coupling: partitioned FV solvers commonly interpolate interface tractions between non-matching meshes, which conserves the total interface force only up to the discretisation error; transferring the loads with the transpose of the motion transfer conserves the force and the interface power~\citep{Farhat1998}, for partitioned and monolithic coupling alike, and in a monolithic system the same weights must also enter the coupling blocks of the preconditioner.

\paragraph{Relation to existing methods.}
The ingredients of the method are, individually, established: the quasi-monolithic architecture, with the fluid mesh motion outside the coupled Newton system, follows \citet{Liu2014} and \citet{JaimanJoshi2022}; JFNK preconditioned by a compact FV approximate Jacobian follows the authors' work on FV solids~\citep{Cardiff2026}; Schur-complement block preconditioning of the coupled system follows the finite element literature~\citep{Heil2004, Deparis2016} and \citet{Eken2016}; and the conservative load transfer is that of \citet{Farhat1998}.
The method differs from the closest monolithic FV scheme, that of \citet{Eken2016}, in discretising both regions with collocated cell-centred FV, with the solid in total Lagrangian velocity form, and in applying the Jacobian matrix-free rather than assembling it.
What is developed here is their combination in a collocated cell-centred discretisation: the interface pressure and traction as boundary values derived from the discrete normal momentum equation and the discrete wall flux, together with evidence of which of these choices the order of accuracy depends on; compact FV linearisations of the interface-coupling blocks for the field-split preconditioner; the conservative transfer applied with the same weights in the residual and in the preconditioner; and an assessment of the resulting solver against partitioned couplings at matched verified error.

Claims that monolithic coupling is more robust or efficient than partitioned coupling are usually supported by iteration counts or run times for a single mesh and time step~\citep{Degroote2009, Heil2008}.
Such comparisons are only meaningful if both solutions are converged to the same tolerance and agree to within their discretisation error.
The companion paper~\citetodo{companion verification paper} assesses partitioned solids4foam solutions of a suite of FSI benchmarks against analytical and independent numerical references, characterises their uncertainty, and introduces a strict coupling convergence test that does not accept unconverged steps in slowly varying phases.
The present paper uses those references as targets and that convergence test for every partitioned comparison, so that the costs of the complete monolithic and partitioned solvers are compared at matched verified error, not the effect of the coupling algorithm alone, since the two solvers also differ in their fluid discretisation and interface conditions (Section~\ref{sec:consistency_partitioned}); it does not re-examine the references themselves.

The specific contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A quasi-monolithic cell-centred FV formulation in which the fluid momentum and continuity equations (in ALE form) and the solid momentum equation (in total Lagrangian velocity form) form a single nonlinear residual with strong enforcement of the interface conditions, solved by JFNK.
    The Jacobian action is evaluated matrix-free, and the assembled approximate Jacobian, built from compact FV linearisations of the fluid, solid and interface-coupling terms, is used only to build a fluid--solid field-split preconditioner; used as the Jacobian, it gives only linear Newton convergence.
    \item An interface treatment for this discretisation, assessed against exact solutions and, for the interface pressure, by comparison with a zero-gradient condition: an interface pressure gradient taken from the normal momentum equation, which gives a second-order added-mass effect where a zero-gradient condition gives first order; an interface traction evaluated as the wall flux of the discrete fluid momentum equation, where a traction recomputed from the deviatoric stress gives first order; and a second-order backward (BDF2) velocity-based solid with no force lag, which adds no measurable numerical damping to the coupling of the ring.
    \item A conservative coupling for non-conformal interfaces, in which the fluid face forces are transferred to the solid with the transpose of the solid-to-fluid velocity transfer~\citep{Farhat1998}, applied with the same weights in the residual and in the preconditioner, so that the total interface force balances to round-off.
    \item An assessment of the complete quasi-monolithic solver against Aitken, IQN-ILS and Robin--Neumann partitioned solvers for robustness, and against IQN-ILS for cost at matched verified error, using the references and the strict coupling convergence test of the companion paper, together with three-dimensional parallel scaling and a report of a case in which the method, like IQN-ILS, requires a smaller time step than that of the partitioned tutorial.
\end{enumerate}
The implementation, built on PETSc (the Portable, Extensible Toolkit for Scientific Computation)~\citep{PETSc}, and all case set-ups are released in solids4foam~\citep{Cardiff2025}.
