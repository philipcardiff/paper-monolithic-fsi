\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}
%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%%
Fluid--solid interaction (FSI) problems in which an incompressible fluid interacts with a light or flexible solid are strongly coupled: the fluid pressure acts on the solid as an added mass, and the coupled problem cannot be advanced reliably by solving the two fields one after the other.
Cell-centred finite volume methods, which store all unknowns at the cell centres (collocated), are widely used for both the fluid and, increasingly, the solid, yet finite volume FSI solvers are mostly partitioned, whereas monolithic and quasi-monolithic Newton-type solvers have been developed predominantly with finite elements, and monolithic finite volume schemes are few~\citep{Eken2016, Eken2017, Tandis2023}.
This paper presents a quasi-monolithic cell-centred finite volume method for FSI, solved with a Jacobian-free Newton--Krylov (JFNK) procedure and implemented in solids4foam~\citep{Cardiff2025}, an open-source solid mechanics and FSI toolbox built on the finite volume library OpenFOAM~\citep{Weller1998}.
It addresses two questions: is the method consistent with the governing equations and with the established partitioned solver of solids4foam, and what does it cost and gain relative to partitioned coupling at the same verified accuracy?

FSI methods can be classified by the modelling viewpoint, the degree of interface coupling and the solver structure.
Eulerian approaches treat both domains as ``fluid-like'', e.g.\ volume-of-fluid~\citep{jain2019conservative} and level-set methods~\citep{dunne2006eulerian, cottet2008eulerian}, and Lagrangian (particle) approaches treat both as ``solid-like''~\citep{aubry2005particle, aubry2006fractional, cremonesi2020state}; the former make sharp, conservative interfaces and general solid constitutive laws difficult, and the latter are typically expensive.
Mixed approaches treat the solid as Lagrangian and the fluid with an arbitrary Lagrangian--Eulerian (ALE) description, which is effective for small to moderate interface motion but fails for very large deformations~\citep{wall2006large}; immersed boundary~\citep{peskin1977numerical, peskin2002immersed, iaccarino2003immersed}, overset~\citep{ge2003three, miller2014overset, li2021fluid, cane2021patient} and cut-cell methods~\citep{pasquariello2016cut, tao2020new, xie2020cartesian} relax this restriction at the price of more complex interface treatment.
The present work uses a body-fitted ALE fluid and a total Lagrangian solid.

Weakly (loosely) coupled schemes solve each field once per time step~\citep{farhat2000two} and are unstable when the added mass is large~\citep{Causin2005, Forster2006}, as for incompressible flow interacting with light, flexible solids.
Strongly coupled schemes enforce the interface kinematic and dynamic conditions at each time step, either by iterating between separate fluid and solid solvers (partitioned coupling) or by solving the fields as one system (monolithic coupling).
Partitioned schemes keep the field solvers and their discretisations separate~\citep{Tukovic2018} and are accelerated by Aitken relaxation~\citep{irons1969version, kuttler2008fixed}, interface quasi-Newton methods such as IQN-ILS (interface quasi-Newton with an approximation of the inverse Jacobian from a least-squares model)~\citep{Degroote2009, bungartz2016precice, Spenke2020, santiago2020hpc, delaisse2022surrogate, Delaisse2023}, or Robin-type interface conditions~\citep{BadiaNobile2008, Tukovic2018b}, which can themselves be accelerated by quasi-Newton methods~\citep{Spenke2023}.
Their convergence deteriorates as the added mass grows.
For an incompressible fluid enclosed entirely by the interface and velocity (Dirichlet) boundaries, standard Dirichlet--Neumann iterations are not even well defined: the fluid sub-problem has a solution only if the interface velocity passed from the solid conserves the enclosed volume, which intermediate iterates in general do not, the ``incompressibility dilemma''~\citep{Kuttler2006}; Robin interface conditions~\citep{BadiaNobile2008, Tukovic2018b}, an additional volume constraint~\citep{Kuttler2006} or monolithic coupling remove this restriction.
Monolithic schemes instead solve the fluid and solid unknowns as one nonlinear system per time step.
They still iterate, typically by Newton's method, but the interface conditions are part of that system rather than enforced by iterating between separate field solvers, so the iterations resolve the nonlinearity of the step and are far less sensitive to the added mass; the price is a loss of modularity and a large, poorly conditioned linear system~\citep{Heil2008}, and where few coupling iterations are needed, well-accelerated partitioned schemes can match or beat their cost~\citep{Degroote2009}.

Monolithic FSI has been developed predominantly with finite elements.
\citet{Heil2004} showed that block-triangular approximations of the coupled Jacobian are effective preconditioners for monolithic ALE FSI solved with GMRES, and \citet{Heil2008} compared monolithic and segregated solution of the same discretisation.
Algebraic multigrid for the complete coupled system was introduced by \citet{Gee2011}, and \citet{Mayr2015} developed a temporally consistent monolithic formulation on that basis; fully implicit Newton--Krylov--Schwarz~\citep{Wu2014} and Newton--multigrid~\citep{Aulisa2018, Failer2020} methods have since been demonstrated, alongside block preconditioners designed for added-mass-dominated problems~\citep{Badia2008, Muddle2012, Deparis2016, Jodlbauer2019, Calandrini2020, Wichrowski2023}.
Semi-implicit schemes treat the interface geometry explicitly while coupling the pressure and the solid implicitly~\citep{Fernandez2007}; in the same spirit, \citet{Liu2014} introduced a quasi-monolithic Galerkin finite element formulation in which the fluid mesh motion is moved outside the coupled Newton system, retaining strong coupling of the fluid and solid unknowns and second-order temporal accuracy under strong added mass; \citet{JaimanJoshi2022} give a textbook account of the resulting algorithm.
The present method adopts this architecture, but discretises both fields with cell-centred finite volumes and retains the nonlinearity of the coupled velocity--pressure system within each time step, which \citet{Liu2014} linearise in time to solve one linear system per step without Newton iterations.
Of the monolithic finite volume schemes, the finite volume--finite element method of \citet{Eken2016, Eken2017} is the closest to the present one in solver architecture: it couples a side-centred (staggered) unstructured finite volume fluid to a Galerkin finite element solid and solves the coupled system in parallel with PETSc, preconditioned by an upper-triangular right preconditioner for the fluid saddle point combined with restricted additive Schwarz.

Body-fitted cell-centred finite volume FSI, particularly within OpenFOAM, has instead mostly followed partitioned coupling; block-coupled multi-region frameworks such as multiRegionFoam~\citep{Alkafri2025} couple interface conditions implicitly but solve the regions with segregated rather than Newton iterations.
The partitioned framework of \citet{Tukovic2018} underpins solids4foam~\citep{Cardiff2025} and much subsequent applied work, and coupling libraries such as preCICE~\citep{bungartz2016precice} extend the same paradigm across codes.
The vertex-centred scheme of \citet{Malan2013} is a strongly coupled partitioned method accelerated within dual-time iteration.
Unified finite volume formulations, beginning with \citet{Greenshields1999b} and \citet{Greenshields2005} and continued by \citet{Papadakis2008}, \citet{Giannopapa2008} and \citet{Tandis2023}, solve common equations across the fluid and solid regions; these are monolithic in that all equations are solved together, but with segregated pressure--velocity algorithms rather than a coupled Newton solve.
For cell-centred finite volume solids, the authors have recently shown that JFNK with a compact approximate Jacobian as preconditioner converges robustly~\citep{Cardiff2026, Batistic2026}; the present work extends that approach to the coupled FSI system.

The finite element literature above leaves three questions open for a monolithic cell-centred finite volume formulation, which this paper answers.
\emph{Which Newton operator?}
Compact finite volume linearisations omit the explicit non-orthogonal corrections and the higher-order reconstructions that the residual contains, so it is not known how well they serve as the Jacobian of the coupled system; we show that, used as the Newton operator, they give only linear convergence, whereas a matrix-free Jacobian action~\citep{Knoll2004} preconditioned by them converges in two to four Newton iterations per step.
\emph{Which interface conditions preserve second-order accuracy?}
In a cell-centred scheme the interface pressure and traction are boundary values reconstructed from cell-centred fields, so the interface boundary condition enters the order of accuracy of the coupled solution whatever the coupling algorithm; the companion verification study traced a first-order displacement convergence in the Turek--Hron FSI3 problem~\citep{Turek2006} partly to a first-order wall-pressure condition~\citep{CardiffFSIVerification2026}.
We show against exact solutions which pressure and traction conditions give second order.
\emph{How should non-conformal interfaces be coupled?}
Interpolating interface tractions between non-matching meshes conserves the total interface force only up to the discretisation error, whereas transferring the loads with the transpose of the motion transfer conserves the force and the interface power~\citep{Farhat1998, deBoer2007}, and mortar formulations achieve consistent non-conforming coupling within monolithic finite element solvers~\citep{Kloppel2011}; in a monolithic system the same transfer must also enter the coupling blocks of the preconditioner, and we show that the interface force then balances to round-off.

\paragraph{Relation to existing methods.}
The architecture follows \citet{Liu2014} and \citet{JaimanJoshi2022}: the fluid mesh motion is outside the coupled system, the solid unknown is the velocity, and the interface velocity continuity is enforced strongly.
The method differs from them in discretising both fields with cell-centred finite volumes rather than finite elements, in enforcing the traction continuity strongly through the interface conditions of the residual rather than in weak form, in retaining the full nonlinearity of the coupled residual within each time step rather than linearising it in time, and in applying the Jacobian matrix-free, with an assembled compact linearisation used only as a preconditioner, following the authors' JFNK finite volume solid solver~\citep{Cardiff2026}.
It differs from the closest monolithic finite volume scheme, that of \citet{Eken2016}, in using a cell-centred rather than side-centred fluid, a finite volume rather than finite element solid in total Lagrangian velocity form, and a matrix-free rather than assembled Jacobian.
The block and Schur-complement preconditioning follows the finite element literature~\citep{Heil2004, Deparis2016} and, for the fluid saddle point, \citet{Eken2016}; the conservative load transfer is that of \citet{Farhat1998}.
What is developed here is their combination in a cell-centred discretisation: the interface pressure and traction as boundary values derived from the discrete normal momentum equation and the discrete wall flux, together with evidence of which of these choices the order of accuracy depends on; compact finite volume linearisations of the interface-coupling blocks for the field-split preconditioner; the conservative transfer applied with the same weights in the residual and in the preconditioner; and an assessment of the resulting solver against partitioned couplings at matched verified error.

Comparisons of monolithic and partitioned coupling~\citep{Heil2008, Degroote2009} are rarely made at matched verified accuracy.
Such comparisons are only meaningful if both solutions are converged to the same tolerance and agree to within their discretisation error.
The companion paper~\citep{CardiffFSIVerification2026} assesses partitioned solids4foam solutions of a suite of FSI benchmarks against analytical and independent numerical references, characterises their uncertainty, and introduces a strict coupling convergence test that does not accept unconverged steps in slowly varying phases.
The present paper uses those references as targets and that convergence test for every partitioned comparison, so that the costs of the complete monolithic and partitioned solvers are compared at matched verified error, not the effect of the coupling algorithm alone, since the two solvers also differ in their fluid discretisation and interface conditions (Section~\ref{sec:consistency_partitioned}); it does not re-examine the references themselves.

The specific contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item A quasi-monolithic cell-centred finite volume formulation in which the fluid momentum and continuity equations (in ALE form) and the solid momentum equation (in total Lagrangian velocity form) form a single nonlinear residual with strong enforcement of the interface conditions, solved by JFNK.
    The Jacobian action is evaluated matrix-free, and the assembled approximate Jacobian, built from compact finite volume linearisations of the fluid, solid and interface-coupling terms, is used only to build a fluid--solid field-split preconditioner; a comparison shows that the same matrix, used instead as the Newton operator, gives only linear convergence, which motivates the matrix-free approach.
    \item An interface treatment for this discretisation, assessed against exact solutions and, for the interface pressure, by comparison with a zero-gradient condition: an interface pressure gradient taken from the normal momentum equation, which gives a second-order added-mass effect where a zero-gradient condition gives first order; an interface traction evaluated as the wall flux of the discrete fluid momentum equation, where a traction recomputed from the deviatoric stress gives first order; and a second-order backward (BDF2) velocity-based solid with no force lag, which adds no measurable numerical damping to the coupled response of the ring.
    \item A conservative coupling for non-conformal interfaces, in which the fluid face forces are transferred to the solid with the transpose of the solid-to-fluid velocity transfer~\citep{Farhat1998}, applied with the same weights in the residual and in the preconditioner, so that the total interface force balances to round-off.
    \item An assessment of the complete quasi-monolithic solver against partitioned Aitken, IQN-ILS and Robin--Neumann couplings for robustness, and against IQN-ILS for cost at matched verified error, using the references and the strict coupling convergence test of the companion paper; a validation against a three-dimensional FSI experiment; three-dimensional parallel scaling; and a three-dimensional flag in air on which both the quasi-monolithic coupling and partitioned IQN-ILS need a smaller time step than that of the solids4foam tutorial.
\end{enumerate}
The implementation, built on PETSc (the Portable, Extensible Toolkit for Scientific Computation)~\citep{PETSc}, and all case set-ups are released publicly in the solids4foam toolbox for OpenFOAM~\citep{Cardiff2025}, inviting the community to explore, extend and compare these procedures.
